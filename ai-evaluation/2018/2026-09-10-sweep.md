## Survivor 1: PWA evidence digest and historical scoring draft

**Identity/date.** The full 16-page [author paper](https://wsp-lab.github.io/papers/son-ppp-ccs18.pdf) identifies CCS 2018, 15–19 October, DOI `10.1145/3243734.3243867`. However the [SIGPL primary programme](https://sigpl.or.kr/school/2018s/) assigns Sooel Son’s Web Security/Privacy lecture to **22 August 2018, 10:00–11:00**, and links the substantive deck. Its PWA half names the paper and authors, diagrams the offline framed-cache oracle and identifies push notifications as the solution to keeping a refactored CoinHive service worker supplied with fresh work. Therefore 22 August is the earliest verified public mechanism date found, not October. The deck also contains 2013 postMessage research, excluded as unrelated old content. The date is an earliest verified bound, not proof that no earlier talk existed.

**Read evidence.** `/tmp/missed-2018/pwa.pdf` and `pwa.txt` (16 pages, 88,097 extracted characters) and `/tmp/missed-2018/pwa-slides.pdf` and `pwa-slides.txt` (46 PDF pages, 6,973 characters). Full extracted candidate text read, including limitations, tables, discussion and references. No historical execution attempted. Some paper diagrams were understood from their captions and corresponding source description rather than pixel-level inspection.

**August contribution available to score.** A malicious offline-capable PWA frames candidate offline-capable sites while network access is unavailable. An observable successful frame load reveals that the victim already holds the target’s local offline content, even though SOP blocks reading the frame. This is an installed local cache-history oracle, not a timing comparison of fresh online responses. A second recipe refactors CoinHive into a service worker and uses push events for wakeup and work distribution, keeping computation possible after leaving the originating page. The deck supplies architectural diagrams, the idle/fresh-work challenge, a measured 24-hour revenue example, and prevalence counts (4,163 service workers, 513 cache-using sites, 3,351 push sites), but not the later paper’s complete compatibility or silent-push implementation details. Consent/offline/background execution restrictions materially limit deployment; do not repeat its broad indefinite-computation/SOP claims unqualified.

**October full-paper supplemental evidence (not borrowed into August score).** Sections 7 and 9.3 identify the concrete iframe `onload` differential in Firefox 59.0.2; other tested browsers fire the event even on failure. Targets must support offline browsing and allow framing; victim must revisit the malicious PWA while offline. Table 5 reports 187/513 identifiable targets, with frame restrictions and broken caches accounting for failures. The paper subsequently says 211, an internal count discrepancy; retain 187 as the explicit table count and do not silently reconcile. Sections 8/8.1 replace unavailable worker WebSocket communications with cross-origin fetch to an attacker proxy and send fresh jobs via push. Firefox permits invisible pushes but revokes after 15 undisplayed messages; periodic resubscription avoids revocation. Edge revokes after 3 and disallows background resubscription; UC lacks required WebAssembly; Chrome displays an update notification. This is why silent mining is specifically demonstrated for Firefox. Cross-browser 24-hour measurements exist, with very low per-worker return, not evidence of an actually deployed large botnet.

Sections 5–6/9 also expose absent notification origin display on specific Firefox/Linux, Firefox/Android and Samsung browser conditions, HTTP-to-HTTPS push-permission delegation confusion, and theft of push subscription capabilities from HTTP or attacker-rewritten HTTP library destinations. Non-VAPID push then accepts forged notifications bearing the trusted subscription origin. Izooto sends subscription keys over HTTP; SendPulse/Pushwoosh use modifiable HTTP-delivered destination configuration despite HTTPS subscription transmission. These are concrete mixed-transport integrity failures, not a TLS break. The exact push-spoofing body was not found in the August deck; its first verified detailed source here is the October paper. Table 4 affected-site counts and prose aggregate differ, so avoid unqualified aggregate reach claims. Generic phishing icons and ordinary bearer-capability theft should not receive independent high originality credit.

**Closest pre-cutoff evidence and differences.**

1. [Lee/Kim/Kim, Identifying Cross-origin Resource Status Using Application Cache](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/01_1_2.pdf), NDSS **8–11 February 2015**, is already listed. Its archived full paper is `archived-references/md/2015/ndss-symposium-identifying-cross-origin-resource-status-using-application-cache.md`; introduction and Sections III–IV were re-read. The attacker creates a manifest naming a cross-origin target and observes success/failure when AppCache fetches it, distinguishing current HTTP status/redirection and deriving login status. The PWA method instead observes whether previously installed origin-bound offline contents satisfy a frame with network unavailable. Both exploit cache/load events, but the probed state, prerequisite and oracle construction differ. Do not claim the first cache privacy oracle.
2. [Chromium Service Worker Security FAQ](https://www.chromium.org/Home/chromium-security/security-faq/service-worker-security-faq/), explicitly frozen with **last update 12 May 2017**, discusses service workers remaining registered, push-triggered later exploitation, and the need for notification permission to get further attacker events after navigation. Local body `/tmp/missed-2018/faq2017.txt`. This is concrete security-use prior art, not merely old API documentation. It defeats a broad claim to first recognize service-worker persistence as an attack channel. The candidate adds the working mining adaptation/work distribution and offline history recipe; October further adds browser-specific silent-push resubscription handling.
3. [Good₿Miner](https://devpost.com/software/good-miner), created **15 October 2017**, explicitly proposes running its browser CoinHive miner in a service worker under “What’s next”. Local body `/tmp/missed-2018/goodminer.txt`. This establishes the general mining idea before 2018, but the primary description labels it future work and does not demonstrate push persistence, post-navigation mining or its restrictions. Do not elevate a one-line plan to the fully developed mechanism.
4. The paper cites [Xu/Zhu’s 2012 smartphone notification phishing](https://www.usenix.org/conference/woot12/workshop-program/presentation/xu) and older timing/cache work. Those precede the general notification-spoofing and cache-existence concepts. Native notification abuse differs from Web push’s third-party HTTPS subscription delegation. These citations are contextual comparisons, not independently full-source-judged survivors.
