### A Perfect CRIME? Only TIME Will Tell — Tal Be'ery and Amichai Shulman

- **Primary:** [33-page whitepaper](https://media.blackhat.com/eu-13/briefings/Beery/bh-eu-13-a-perfect-crime-beery-wp.pdf), [original event abstract](https://blackhat.com/archive/europe/2013/briefings.html), [event schedule](https://www.blackhat.com/docs/eu-13/eu-13-sok.pdf).
- **Date:** Black Hat Europe, March 2013; the primary program identifies the talk as T204. Exact day and whether the technical whitepaper appeared slightly before the event are not established here. Use March 2013 as the defensible cutoff interval; do not infer an exact first-publication day from the URL. Year confidence high; exact-cutoff confidence medium.
- **Reading:** complete whitepaper text, including Appendix A's recovery log and Appendix B's JavaScript, read through the web PDF reader. Direct shell downloads returned 403, so no local source PDF is claimed. Screenshot retrieval was inconsistent; figures are not independently validated as images. No exploit was run.
- **Evidence digest:** The demonstrated timing primitive tunes request size around a TCP acknowledgement boundary, making one byte produce an extra round trip. The CNN JavaScript experiment distinguishes parameter lengths 2588/2589; cross-origin rejection ends observation after headers. Separately, a Google Scholar response-compression experiment recovers an email address by observing response lengths and iterating reflected guesses. Section 4.3 explicitly leaves their combination into timing-based compressed-response secret extraction as future work. Thus the paper demonstrates useful constituent methods, not an end-to-end networkless response-secret attack.
- **Distinctness:** the candidate's useful extension is the concrete byte-length-to-RTT measurement route. Merely observing compression length, choosing matching plaintext, or timing a cross-origin resource is earlier knowledge. BREACH is already listed in 2013, but does not represent this separately demonstrated timing route; later work cannot decide novelty at TIME's cutoff.

### Precise technical comparison

| Dimension | Ling, INFOCOM 2012 | TIME, March 2013 |
|---|---|---|
| Attacker observation | Passive packet timestamps and lengths on the client–SSH-proxy path. | Browser JavaScript measures cross-origin XHR completion/error time. |
| Signal construction | Pair HTTP request/response activity through the tunnel; estimate network RTT. | Choose request lengths on either side of a sender acknowledgement boundary; one byte can add an RTT. |
| Inference | Train per-site RTT profiles; use kernel density estimation and Bayesian classification on sample mean/variance to identify the visited site. | Demonstrate timing separation for known 2588/2589-byte parameters. Compression-based secret recovery is a separate length-observation experiment. |
| Capability and output difference | Packet capture and prior profiles; website classification. | Attacker-chosen browser requests; a byte-length-to-time measurement primitive. |

Ling's sections III–VI do not supply the chosen-byte browser oracle, adaptive compression guessing or an ACK-boundary experiment. Its reported 98.7% result is top-10 identification among 100 sites, not 98.7% exact-site identification. Its proxy-delay defenses equalize RTT statistics. These full-source details establish a related timing channel with a different mechanism; they do not remove TIME's narrower contribution.

The [2007 Bortz–Boneh–Nandy paper](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/pubs/papers/webtiming.pdf) was recovered locally as `webtiming2007.pdf/.txt` and its relevant methods reopened. Browser cross-origin timing, header/error observations, and minimum-sample noise filtering remain prior knowledge. Section 3.1, footnote 2 explicitly excludes multi-packet requests from its experiments. TIME receives no originality credit for these general browser-timing ingredients.

Fresh backward search also located [Gilad and Herzberg, Spying in the Dark: TCP and Tor Traffic Analysis](https://www.freehaven.net/anonbib/cache/tcp-tor-pets12.pdf), PETS 2012; the [authors' institutional publication record](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/spying-in-the-dark-tcp-and-tor-traffic-analysis/) confirms the July 2012 conference. Local copies: `gilad2012.pdf/.txt`. This comparison read the threat model and section 4 timing attack, not every page of that 20-page paper. Spoofed TCP probes test candidate connection tuples: a successful probe induces traffic that delays a subsequent timing query, enabling connection/port discovery. This is important pre-cutoff active TCP timing work, but requires different packet-sending capabilities and does not demonstrate TIME's chosen HTTP byte boundary. Accordingly, avoid calling TIME the first TCP timing attack or the first attack without direct traffic observation.

### CRIME and demonstration limits clarified

All 43 slides of the [original linked CRIME presentation](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/11eBmGiHbYcHR9gL5nDyZChu_-lCa2GizeuOfaLU2HOU/export/pdf) were recovered and read as `crime2012.pdf/.txt`. TIME's own footnote 10 links this exact presentation ID, establishing a contemporary primary connection. The current Google export is mutable; this pass did not authenticate an immutable 2012 slide snapshot. Nevertheless the recovered slides are contrary evidence to any confident claim that CRIME considered requests alone: slides 38–39 explicitly discuss response gzip, reflected input, and extraction of PII/XSRF tokens. They describe the proposed algorithm as not yet tested. Earlier slides also discuss compression-window and TLS-record boundaries; these are not a browser TCP-ACK timing experiment.

TIME therefore gets **no credit for first proposing reflected-response compression extraction**. Its Google Scholar length-based recovery log supplies an experiment rather than a new general compression principle. The surviving scored advance is its concrete TCP-boundary browser measurement.

The full TIME source also imposes a narrower demonstration scope than the abstract might suggest:

- Appendix B calls `http://edition.cnn.com/?`, uses the two hard-coded parameter lengths, and times XHR with `Date.getTime()`. This is an HTTP experiment; it does not establish a working HTTPS compressed-response secret oracle.
- Request length is already known in that timing experiment. It demonstrates signal discrimination, not recovery of an unknown browser secret.
- Section 4.2 observes response lengths for the separate Google Scholar experiment. Section 4.3 explicitly places their combination in future work. No full combined exploit, automated boundary discovery, or broad deployment success is credited.

No exploit was executed. Full text was read; this follow-up did not independently inspect every figure as an image. TIME remains dated to March 2013, without an invented exact first-publication day. Later BREACH, HEIST and surveys are not evidence of its publication-time value.
