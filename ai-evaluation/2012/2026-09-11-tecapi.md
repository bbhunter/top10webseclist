# TECAPI follow-up — 2012

Bounded review of AJAX Hammer and the subsequently identified Diviner lead. The
2012 nomination and missed-entry lists were read, and the 97-URL exclusion set
was generated with `known_links.py 2012 --raw`. Archive files were searched by
`dynamic CSRF`, `XMLHttpRequest`, `intranet zone`, cross-domain response access,

# Research Evaluation

## Candidate

- **Title:** AJAX Hammer — Harnessing AJAX for Dynamic CSRF
- **Author or organisation:** Oren Ofer, Hacktics Advanced Security Center, Ernst & Young. The cover names Ofer; Eran Tamari is an additional contributor, not a cover coauthor.
- **Publication date:** 9 January 2012, dated original whitepaper; exact initial upload time unverified.
- **Reference:** [Original 17-page whitepaper preserved by Google Code](https://storage.googleapis.com/google-code-archive-downloads/v2/code.google.com/hasc-research/AJAX%20Hammer%20-%20Harnessing%20AJAX%20for%20%28Direct%29%20Dynamic%20CSRF.pdf).

## Core Contribution

An attacker-controlled intranet page can exploit permissive browser zone settings
and an affirmative cross-domain warning to use credentialed XMLHttpRequest against
other origins. The paper demonstrates response reads and custom request headers,
then explains adaptive request forgery using returned tokens or ViewState and
requests with bodies and content types unavailable to ordinary HTML forms. This
is a concrete adaptation of known dynamic CSRF to zone-authorized response access,
not a discovery of CSRF or a silent bypass of browser consent.

Verified from the complete paper and its page-10 screenshot: the example sends
cookies to a simulated target and renders its returned content under a different
origin. The target is a search page, not a demonstrated account compromise.
Token extraction follows from the demonstrated read primitive but an end-to-end
token-protected exploit is not reproduced in the paper. Safari 4 coverage and
users' likelihood of accepting warnings are author claims; the latter is expressly
extrapolated from an unrelated SSL-warning experiment. The paper states matching
protocol and port, intranet classification, enabled zone settings, authentication,
and user confirmation as conditions.

## Prior Art

- **Novelty cutoff:** 9 January 2012, the complete whitepaper's cover date. Oren Hafif's [publication list](https://www.orenhafif.com/p/publications.html) independently identifies the work as 2012; his [contributor record](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hafif) gives 9 January. The original OWASP meeting page and exact upload timestamp were not recovered, so confidence in the day is lower than confidence in the year.
- **Earliest credible pre-cutoff related work:** Microsoft's [MS02-023 bulletin](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security-updates/securitybulletins/2002/ms02-023), May 2002, already describes intranet-zone cross-domain access prompting. This establishes old component behavior, not the complete adaptive-CSRF method. [Amit Klein's original disclosure](https://seclists.org/bugtraq/2005/Sep/286), 24 September 2005, reads cross-domain responses using malformed XHR requests through a proxy/shared host, but explicitly says target cookies and authentication are absent.
- **Closest pre-cutoff equivalent work:** [Hamiel and Moyer, Dynamic Cross-Site Request Forgery](https://www.blackhat.com/presentations/bh-usa-09/HAMIEL/BHUSA09-Hamiel-DynamicCSRF-PAPER.pdf), 24 July 2009, already describes per-user token capture and adaptive forgery through referrer leakage, token fixation, and related channels; AJAX Hammer cites it. [Yorick Koster's June 2006 disclosure](https://www.akitasecurity.nl/advisory/AK20060601/internet_explorer_redirect_arbitrary_file_access_vulnerability.html) supplies a different XHR response-read route through double redirects, including an intranet-only IE7 case. Neither supplies this exact ordinary-XHR, user-approved zone route plus the documented custom-header/request-format testing workflow. [Eric Law's own 13 June 2011 answer](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/6290149/how-to-overcome-this-security-issue) confirms that intranet XHR prompts were already publicly understood.
- **Local comparison:** [DNS Pinning Just Got Worse](../../archived-references/md/2006/ha-ckers-org-dns-pinning-just-got-worse.md) explicitly reads unauthenticated intranet pages through rebinding; [Cross Environment Hopping](../../archived-references/md/2008/ibm-application-security-insider-cross-environment-hopping.md) uses localhost XSS, cross-port access and a proxy; [DNS Poisoning via Port Exhaustion](../../archived-references/md/2011/2011-ibm-application-security-insider-dns-poisoning-port-exhaustion.md) uses zone classification for NTLM and clipboard access. These are related mechanisms, not evidence that this candidate's particular credentialed XHR adaptation was already nominated.
- **Evidence boundary:** All six scores use the dated January whitepaper and pre-cutoff material. [Top-Level Universal XSS](https://superevr.com/blog/2012/top-level-universal-xss), already nominated in 2012, is dated 30 August 2012 and therefore excluded as prior art. It adds the distinct internet-hosted bare-TLD route into the intranet zone. Later browser obsolescence, patches and uptake do not affect scores. The later author bibliography is used only to corroborate attribution/year.
- **Candidate's distinct contribution as of the cutoff:** A practical intranet-origin adaptation of dynamic CSRF with victim credentials and direct response analysis, explaining why request-format and custom-header assumptions fail under this browser policy. The exact zone behavior is old; the demonstrated security workflow is the modest added contribution. No exact predecessor found is not proof of first invention.

## Reverification

- **Candidate facts rechecked against:** Full 17-page PDF; independently rendered page 10 confirmed the warning, cookie-bearing request, and foreign response display. Page 13 confirms the form-format comparison. Cover and credits correct the preliminary attribution to Oren Ofer.
- **Cutoff audit:** Hamiel/Moyer's cover is July 2009; Klein's original mailing-list date is September 2005; Koster's original page is June 2006; the vendor bulletin is from 2002; Law's answer is explicitly June 2011. Superevr's August 2012 page was reopened and excluded from novelty scoring.
- **Independent prior-art check:** A second search by the warning text and `Access data sources across domains`, rather than by CSRF or AJAX Hammer, found the 2011 developer explanation; a cross-origin response-read search found Koster's redirect attack. Reopened primary sources confirmed that known APIs and related response-read exploits must constrain originality without proving equivalence of the complete workflow.
- **Strongest challenge to the result:** Both dynamic CSRF and intranet cross-origin XHR were public knowledge, and a later nomination shares the same response-read sink. The candidate warrants only incremental combination credit; it does not establish invention of either component or a warning-free browser exploit.
- **Benefit-of-doubt check:** It demonstrates a reusable credentialed read channel with application-level consequences under explicit conditions. Neither MonkeyFist's indirect leakage nor Klein's unauthenticated responses supplies precisely that channel.
- **Changes after reverification:** Kept the 60.3 score after checking the prior-art relationships. Narrowed demonstrated impact to the actual simulated request/response evidence, corrected authorship, and excluded later UXSS from the historical score. Did not carry the paper's single-quoted "JSON" example or log-size random-seed recommendation forward as endorsed guidance.

## Verdict

**Primary verdict: Meaningful combination or adaptation**

- **Archive decision:** Include as a supporting reference.
- **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Reasoning:** Qualifies for the historical missed-work gate at 60.3, with a modest distinct security adaptation. It is the earlier direct-CSRF research, while the already nominated August work contributes bare-TLD zone entry. Preserve and identify the January paper without duplicating the later TLD claim.
- **Evidence gaps:** Exact original upload/meeting timestamp, independent execution on historical browsers, complete browser matrix, and the externally linked end-to-end demonstration were not verified. None is silently treated as proof of novelty or lack of value.

# Research Evaluation

## Candidate

- **Title:** The Diviner — Clairvoyance in the Digital Frontier
- **Author or organisation:** Shay Chen and Eran Tamari, Hacktics ASC, Ernst & Young.
- **Publication date:** 30 July 2012 author article and initial source release; predecessor demonstration images were uploaded 29 July.
- **Reference:** [Original article](https://sectooladdict.blogspot.com/2012/07/the-diviner-clairvoyance-in-digital.html); [original v1.0.1 beta source](https://storage.googleapis.com/google-code-archive-downloads/v2/code.google.com/diviner/diviner-1.0.1beta-src.zip).

## Core Contribution

Automate cross-page experiments and expose inferred server state to a human
tester. The article describes a ZAP extension that varies parameters, login,
request history and sessions, then classifies observable effects. It produces
leads and hypothetical code, not stolen source. This is useful tooling for
indirect attacks, with incomplete heuristics and limited demonstrated scale.

Static inspection of the actual first source release confirms the core:
`DatabaseAnalyzer` seeds an input, repeats a scenario omitting its source page,
and checks whether an effect persists; `CodeBehaviour` maps classifications to
templates; `Clairvoyance` supplies fixed confidence values and ordering. These
are analyst-facing hypotheses, not recovered database schemas or measured
probabilities. The release explicitly comments on incomplete insert-case handling.
No source code or exploit was executed.

## Prior Art

- **Novelty cutoff:** 29–30 July 2012. [Preserved download metadata](https://storage.googleapis.com/google-code-archive/v2/code.google.com/diviner/downloads-page-1.json) records demonstration images on 29 July and the initial source release on 30 July at 19:31:27 UTC (upload 19:32:39). The latter is labeled the initial public release; the article is dated 30 July. The source archive's SHA-1 matches metadata: `39ff8219c544f3b1be5b9321fddf3a7432a8b82e`. The project's February creation time establishes no technical disclosure by itself. No evidence in the one-day interval is used as prior art.
- **Earliest credible pre-cutoff related work:** [Galán, Alcaide, Orfila and Blasco, A Multi-agent Scanner to Detect Stored-XSS Vulnerabilities](https://www.jorgeblascoalis.com/assets/papers/galan2010multi.pdf), November 2010, already automates injecting inputs and searching other pages for their later appearance. Date independently confirmed by the [authors' university record](https://e-archivo.uc3m.es/entities/publication/06283085-4b93-4c0c-a1d7-112d96cbec2e). It detects stored XSS rather than inferring shared storage lifetime and displaying generic behavior hypotheses.
- **Closest pre-cutoff equivalent work:** Chen's [Session Puzzles whitepaper](https://storage.googleapis.com/google-code-archive-downloads/v2/code.google.com/puzzlemall/Session%20Puzzles%20-%20Indirect%20Application%20Attack%20Vectors%20-%20May%202011%20-%20Whitepaper.pdf), May 2011, already tells black-box testers to map which entry points set, use and overwrite shared session values and vary request sequences. [BLOCK, Li and Xue](https://www.acsac.org/2011/program-files/s151.html), ACSAC December 2011, already infers behavioral invariants from request/response and session observations for runtime state-violation detection. Neither makes ordinary behavior inference or indirect input effects novel in 2012. Diviner adds an active experiment-and-explanation workflow for testers, rather than BLOCK's attack-free learning and runtime enforcement.
- **Local comparison:** Read the preserved [Session Puzzles](../../archived-references/md/2011/session-puzzles-indirect-application-attack-vectors-may-2011-whitepaper.md) detection guidance and [BLOCK record](../../archived-references/md/2011/ptolemy-berkeley-edu-block-black-box-approach-detection-state-applications.md). The already-listed [Enemy of the State](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity12/technical-sessions/presentation/doupe) focuses on inferred state-machine crawling and state-aware vulnerability fuzzing, a different output from Diviner's cross-page behavior map. Its verified conference disclosure is August 2012; an earlier technical release was not established, so it is excluded from historical scoring.
- **Evidence boundary:** Original July article plus v1.0.1 source, not the later project homepage's ZAP 2.x capabilities or 2013 releases. No later bug fixes, usage, or adoption affect any category. The subsequent November Diviner talk is not used to backfill July implementation claims.
- **Candidate's distinct contribution as of the cutoff:** Incremental automation and presentation of active input-to-output experiments, including persistent versus session-scoped effects, to prioritize manual testing. It is a methodology/tooling contribution rather than a new vulnerability class or general source-code extraction method.

## Reverification

- **Candidate facts rechecked against:** Reopened the original article and read `Clairvoyance.java`, `CodeBehaviour.java`, `DatabaseAnalyzer.java` and `GenericAnalyzer.java` from the checksum-verified first source ZIP. No arbitrary filesystem extraction or execution was used.
- **Cutoff audit:** Download metadata distinguishes July 2012 artifacts from December 2012/2013 revisions. BLOCK's conference record and Galán's university record verify pre-cutoff publication. Session Puzzles states its May 2011 disclosure. Later state-aware scanning papers are not treated as earlier simply because they share the 2012 directory.
- **Independent prior-art check:** Repeated the search through stored-XSS scanner architecture and black-box session-variable inference. This found Galán's 2010 scanning pipeline and confirmed BLOCK as a close conceptual predecessor. The draft broad-novelty reading did not survive that check.
- **Strongest challenge to the result:** Much of the reasoning already exists in Session Puzzles and black-box modeling. The source's template probabilities are constants, and persistent effects do not uniquely identify database operations. Calling this arbitrary source recovery would be wrong.
- **Benefit-of-doubt check:** A useful inference tool need not recover actual source. Its verified experiment implementation can still expose useful hypotheses and make existing manual methods more systematic.
- **Changes after reverification:** Settled on tooling credit at 62.2 after reviewing the initial implementation, narrowed the cutoff to a one-day interval, and excluded future code-ordering promises. The score reflects demonstrated tooling rather than headline claims.

## Verdict

**Primary verdict: Tooling or methodology contribution**

- **Archive decision:** Include as a supporting reference.
- **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Reasoning:** Meets the historical 55-point gate as incremental tooling. It complements the existing session-puzzling and state-aware-scanning records without claiming a new attack class.
- **Evidence gaps:** No independent execution or performance/accuracy study was performed; screenshot publication may precede the detailed explanation by one day. These gaps limit confidence and the claims preserved with the entry.
